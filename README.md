## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

A piece of text takes this path from arriving to the label a reader sees:

1. **`POST /submit` (`app.py`)** receives `{text, creator_id}`. It rejects missing, empty or too-short text and assigns a `content_id`. Long text is accepted, but only the first 400 tokens are scored.
2. **Model signal (`detector.py`)** runs the text through a local GPT-2 and adds `model_score`, which measures how predictable the text is.
3. **Style signal (`stylometry.py`)** measures sentence-length spread, vocabulary repetition, punctuation and personal voice (first-person words and contractions), and adds `style_score`.
4. **Combining rule (`scoring.py::combine_signals`)** merges the two scores into one 0–1 score, where higher means more likely AI.
5. **Labeller (`scoring.py::score_to_label`)** compares that score to the thresholds in `config.py` and adds a guess (`ai`, `human` or `unsure`) and a plain-language sentence for the reader.
6. **Audit log (`audit.py`)** writes the `content_id`, both signal scores, the combined score and the label to `logs/audit.jsonl`. This record is what `POST /appeal` later looks up.
7. **Response** returns JSON to the writer with `content_id`, `guess`, `confidence`, `label`, `model_score` and `style_score`.

### Endpoint shapes

**`POST /submit`**

| | Field | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| in | `text` | string, required | The writing to check. Must be non-empty. Signal one reads only the first 400 tokens (about 300 words) and ignores the rest |
| in | `creator_id` | string, required | Who is submitting |
| out | `content_id` | string | Unique ID for this decision, used by `/appeal` |
| out | `guess` | `"ai"`, `"human"` or `"unsure"` | Machine-readable result |
| out | `confidence` | float, 0–1 | The combined score, where higher means more likely AI |
| out | `label` | string | The plain-language sentence a reader sees |
| out | `model_score` | float, 0–1 | Signal one |
| out | `style_score` | float, 0–1 | Signal two |

Bad input returns `400` with `{"error": "<code>", "message": "<plain sentence>"}`.

**`POST /appeal`**

| | Field | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| in | `content_id` | string, required | The ID returned by `/submit` |
| in | `reasoning` | string, required | Why the writer says the label is wrong |
| out | `content_id` | string | Echoed back |
| out | `status` | `"under_review"` | The new status |
| out | `message` | string | Confirmation for the writer |

An unknown `content_id` returns `404` with the same error shape as `/submit`. The writer's `creator_id` is not sent. The handler looks it up from the original log entry with `audit.entries_for(content_id)`.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is. A local GPT-2 reads the text word by word, in context, and scores how surprised it is by each next word. This is a judgment about the content: which words fit here, given what came before.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** AI text is generated by picking likely next words, so a language model finds it easy to predict. Human writers more often choose a rare word or an unexpected turn.

**What it can't see:** Authorship. It sees predictability, and plain writing is predictable too. Second-language writers, younger writers and anyone who sticks to common words in short sentences score as machine-like. Dense or ornate human prose scores as human, because rare words are what the model fails to predict.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** The shape of the text, without reading it: sentence-length spread (weight 0.40), vocabulary variety (0.15), punctuation density (0.15), and personal voice, meaning first-person words and contractions (0.30). Text under 15 words or 2 sentences scores a neutral 0.5.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** AI prose tends toward even, mid-length sentences and a narrow vocabulary. Human writing is lumpier, with a three-word sentence next to a forty-word one.

**What it can't see:** Meaning. Anything that changes the shape without changing the substance moves it, such as typos, chopped-up sentences or a pasted quotation. It's cheap to do on purpose.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** Average the two signals. If they differ by more than 0.4, subtract 0.1, so a split leans "human". Wrongly accusing a writer costs more than missing AI text.

**The numbers:** weights 0.5 / 0.5, gap 0.4, penalty 0.1 (in `config.py`). My first version used the lower score on a split, but one weak signal then dragged everything down and no text could reach the AI label.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**

A polished AI paragraph scored model 0.76 but style 0.38, because the style signal read its varied vocabulary as human. The gap is under 0.4, so the rule averages them to **0.57** ("unsure").

| Input | model | style | combined |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clearly AI | 0.76 | 0.38 | 0.57 |
| Clearly human | 0.09 | 0.08 | 0.08 |
| Formal human | 0.47 | 0.20 | 0.34 |
| Lightly-edited AI | 0.36 | 0.04 | 0.20 |

No human text was accused. The edited AI was missed: swapping stock phrases for first-person words fools the style signal.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – 0.35 | "We think this was probably written by a person." |
| unsure | 0.35 – 0.65 | "We can't tell for sure whether this was written by a person or by AI." |
| high-confidence AI | 0.65 – 1.00 | "This looks like it may have been written with AI. We could be wrong. If you wrote it yourself, you can appeal and a person will review it." |

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**
It says plainly that we don't know, and it doesn't say anything about the writer. The person reading it may have written every word themselves, so it avoids words like "suspicious" or "flagged". The AI label is the one that can hurt, so it hedges ("may have", "we could be wrong") and points to the appeal.

## How I Used AI

**Moment 1**

- *What I asked for:* A combining rule for my two signals that avoids falsely accusing writers.
- *What came back:* "Use the lower score when the signals differ by more than 0.4."
- *What I changed:* I ran it on four test inputs and nothing reached the AI label, because the weak style signal dragged every score down. I replaced it with the average minus 0.1 on a split.

**Moment 2**

- *What I asked for:* A fix for my style signal, which scored obvious AI text as human.
- *What came back:* Extra checks, including a list of stock AI phrases.
- *What I changed:* I removed the stock-phrase check. I kept the personal-voice check and reweighted the other measures.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 10 per minute, 200 per day

**Why those numbers and not others:**
A writer revising their work rarely submits more than 3–5 times a minute, so 10
leaves room. A script mapping my thresholds needs hundreds of inputs: 10/minute
throttles it fast, and 200/day stops it from just sending slowly all day.

**What a caller counts as:** Per `creator_id`, falling back to IP address when
there isn't one. People sharing a network don't share a limit. The weakness is
that `creator_id` is self-reported, so a script can send a new one each time and
dodge the limit. Per-IP would catch that, but would lump together everyone
behind one shared address.
